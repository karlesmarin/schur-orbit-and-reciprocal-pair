# 🧩 How the columns fit together — code and data (Part II)

Companion material for *The order generated by a column of the $\mathrm{SU}(n)_k$ $S$-matrix is
locally a numerical semigroup ring. Part II: the gluing term, the distribution of the defect, and
the classical objects at its two ends* (Carles Marín), deposited on Zenodo:
[doi:10.5281/zenodo.22864266](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22864266). That is the **concept
DOI** — it always resolves to the current version — and it carries the note in both editions,
English and Spanish.

Both PDFs are here too — `P3b.pdf` and `P3b_es.pdf`, byte for byte the files deposited on Zenodo —
together with the 31 scripts behind every number the note calls *measured* and their archived

It sits beside [`s-matrix-column/`](../s-matrix-column) because it is the sequel to it, and it

## 🎯 The question

Part I settled each column on its own: at prime height $p=n+k$ the ring $A_\mu$ is, locally at
$p$, a monomial ring whose valuations form a numerical semigroup $S_T$, and the index is $p$ to
the number of gaps $\delta(T)$. This note asks how the columns sit **together**. The invariant
that answers such a question is older than the question — the discriminant — and its $p$-adic
valuation splits into ramification, twice the sum of the column defects, and twice a gluing term.
The gluing has a closed form by orbits except for one census,

$$\Delta(n,p)\;=\;\sum_{\text{orbits}}\delta(T),$$

and that census is what the note computes. Since $\delta$ depends only on which power sums of the
spectrum vanish, it is a question about vanishing moments from end to end.

## 📐 What the note proves

1. **The small defects are classified.** For a centred spectrum with trivial stabiliser,
   $\delta\ge2$ is governed by two single vanishing conditions and $\delta\ge3$ by exactly four
   *pairs* of them — one for each numerical semigroup of genus three. That those four semigroups
   are the only ones is classical; the moment condition attached to each is what is new here.
2. **Almost every column has defect one.** The proportion with $\delta\ge2$ is
   $2/p-1/p^{2}+O_n(p^{1-n/2}+p^{1+\lfloor n/2\rfloor-n})$ for each fixed $n\ge5$, with no
   factorial threshold, from a character-sum bound whose constant is written out.
3. **The mean defect per orbit** is $1+2/p+3/p^{2}+O_n(p^{-\min(3,n/2-1)})$, and the $3$ is
   $-1+4$: the $-1$ of the first expansion, and one unit for each of the four semigroups.
4. **A central regime.** For $\varepsilon p\le n\le(1-\varepsilon)p$ both expansions hold
   uniformly, the first with an exponentially small error.

## 🏛️ The two ends are classical, and nobody built them for this

At the **top** of the census, the columns of maximal defect are exactly the totally split
Bring–Jerrard trinomials $X^n+aX+b$ with $ab\ne0$ — the normal form Bring reduced the general
quintic to in 1786 — counted for $n=5$ by the elliptic curve of conductor $50$. There the
Hasse–Weil bound is quadratically miscalibrated: the first column of maximal defect occurs at
$p=67$, $163$ and $601$ for $n=5,6,7$, all **below** $n!$.

Deep in the distribution, one of the four genus-three families is realised by the **half-systems**
of Gauss's lemma, the device Gauss built in 1801 to prove quadratic reciprocity. They give an
infinite family of non-Gorenstein orders whose value semigroup $\langle3,5,7\rangle$ does not move
with $p$. Separately, the centred half-systems are counted by
$H(p)=\bigl(2^{(p-1)/2}+(2\mid p)(p-1)\bigr)/p$ — a closed form for a sequence catalogued since
1965, and an exact relabelling of the Fermat quotient of $2$.

## 🧾 What is measured and what is not

Two statements carry no proof and say so where they appear: the trichotomy, and the closed form
for the gluing, which is verified computationally rather than derived. Everything resting on the
second inherits that status, and the estimate of $G(n,p)$ is stated as conditional on it.

One theorem is explicitly **not** confirmed by computation, and the note says so rather than
dressing it up: at $p\le19$ the ratio $\Delta/b$ runs between $0.50$ and $1.01$ where the
expansion predicts about $1.11$. The computed range is nowhere near the asymptotic regime. That
theorem rests on its proof and nothing else.
